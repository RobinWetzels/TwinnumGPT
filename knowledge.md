## Why do people communicate? What are the five motives for communication?

Communication has five primary motives, which align with Maslow's hierarchy of needs: physical, instrumental, relational, identity, and spiritual needs.

- Physical needs: Communication supports mental and physical health and fosters connections that enhance well-being.
- Instrumental and task needs: It helps people complete practical tasks essential for survival and safety.
- Relational needs: It helps establish and maintain relationships, providing companionship and affection.
- Identity needs: Interactions shape self-perception and identity, influenced by context and audience.
- Spiritual needs: It addresses existential questions and fulfills spiritual longings through various forms of expression.

## How does communication work? What is the semantic triangle and what are the models of communication?

Communication involves symbols, which are interpreted through a semantic triangle. The triangle has three parts: the referent (the actual object), the symbol (the representation), and thought (the connection between the two).

There are three models of the communication process:

- Action model: A linear, one-way process in which a sender transmits a message to a receiver.
- Interaction model: A two-way process that includes feedback and context.
- Transactional model: A simultaneous process in which both parties act as senders and receivers.

## What are the characteristics of language and the types of metaphors?

Language is a symbol system that is essential for human relationships. It is complex and can lead to misunderstandings if used incorrectly. It has four characteristics:

- Arbitrary: Language symbols have no inherent connection to what they represent. For example, the word "selfie" is a label agreed upon by a community.
- Ambiguous: Words can have multiple meanings depending on context, which can lead to misunderstandings.
- Abstract: Language refers to intangible concepts and ideas, not just physical objects. The more specific the language, the clearer the communication.
- Negative: Language creates distinctions between what something is and what it is not, which can clarify meaning.

Metaphors come in six types:

- Simile: Compares two things using "like" or "as."
- Synecdoche: Uses a part to represent the whole.
- Metonym: Uses tangible objects to refer to intangible concepts.
- Archetypal: Draws from common human experiences.
- Mixed: Combines unrelated metaphors.
- Dead: Common phrases that have lost their original metaphorical meaning.

## What are the types of nonverbal communication and the guidelines for using it?

There are eight types of nonverbal communication:

1. Kinesics: Body movements, gestures, and posture.
2. Oculesics: Eye contact and its implications.
3. Proxemics: Use of personal space and distance.
4. Haptics: Communication through touch.
5. Chronemics: Perception and use of time.
6. Olfactics: Communication through smell.
7. Vocalics: Nonverbal elements of voice, such as tone and volume.
8. Artifacts: Objects and personal items that convey information about identity and values.

Guidelines for dialogic nonverbal communication:

- Don't assume others understand your nonverbal expressions.
- Control your nonverbal reactions so they align with your verbal messages.
- Use situationally appropriate nonverbal expressions.
- Learn cultural differences in meaning.

## What is a formal presentation? What are Aristotle's three types of speeches and five canons of rhetoric?

A formal presentation is a form of public communication in which a presenter speaks to an audience for an extended period without interruption. The origins of public speaking can be traced back to Ancient Greece and Rome.

Aristotle categorized speeches into three types:

- Deliberative: about future actions.
- Forensic: about past events.
- Epideictic: celebratory.

He also introduced the five canons of rhetoric: invention (topic identification), arrangement (order of points), style (language use), delivery (physical and vocal aspects), and memory (recall of the speech).

## How do I choose a speech topic and analyze my audience?

Choosing a topic is the first step in preparing a formal presentation. The general purpose of a speech can be to inform, persuade, or commemorate. Appropriate topics depend on the occasion and audience. For example, wedding toasts should focus on the couple, while commencement speeches often address themes of success. The topic should also suit the speaker, reflecting their interests and expertise, and it must be narrowed to fit the time constraints of the presentation.

Understanding the audience is vital. Audience analysis can use demographics (age, sex, etc.), artifacts (objects reflecting values), informants (contacts within the group), interviews, surveys, polling, and direct observation. This information helps tailor the speech to the audience's interests and knowledge level.

## How do I research a speech and evaluate sources?

Research is a critical component of speech preparation. Reliable sources include peer-reviewed academic articles, books, magazines, web sources, and interviews with experts.

Supporting evidence can take various forms: statistics, examples, testimony, and definitions.

When evaluating sources, check each source's:

- Recency
- Credibility
- Potential bias
- Consistency with other sources

## How do I organize a speech? What are transitions, outlines, and citations?

Effective organization of main points and evidence enhances the clarity and coherence of a presentation. Organizing involves moving from a broad topic to a specific purpose and thesis statement.

Transitions connect different parts of the speech and help guide the audience. They include internal summaries, signposts, and internal previews.

Two types of outlines are used: a full-sentence preparation outline, which includes everything planned for the speech, and a key word speaking outline, which contains key words to remind the speaker of main points and supporting details.

Citing sources is essential for credibility. Cite sources verbally in the speech, in presentation aids, and in the references page of the outline. Each citation should include the source's name, publication date, and credentials.

## What is dialogic public speaking?

Dialogic public speaking creates an environment that encourages audience engagement. It fosters a more interactive and thoughtful exchange of ideas. Speakers should:

- Identify with their audience
- Respect differences
- Keep an open mind
- Strive for understanding
- Maintain a conversational tone

Organization can also encourage dialogue through ethical attention getters, establishing personal credibility, providing source credentials, allowing audience evaluation, and acknowledging disagreement.

## What are the components and forms of speech delivery?

Delivery is one of the five canons of rhetoric and often influences how an audience perceives and retains a message. Good delivery captures attention, enhances credibility, and makes messages memorable, while poor delivery can undermine even well-prepared speeches.

Vocal delivery (vocalics) includes pronunciation, articulation, volume, pitch, rhythm, rate, tone, and vocalized pauses. Physical delivery includes physical appearance, posture, facial expressions, gestures, and eye contact. Cultures have different expectations for attire and gestures.

There are four forms of delivery:

- Memorized: The speaker commits the entire speech to memory. This allows maximum eye contact and gestures, but it is time-consuming and can lead to freezing or forgetting content.
- Manuscript: The speaker reads the speech verbatim. This allows precise wording but limits eye contact and can sound unnatural.
- Extemporaneous: The speaker uses a limited outline, allowing natural delivery and audience adaptation, but it requires significant preparation.
- Impromptu: The speaker delivers a speech with little or no preparation, often in response to questions or prompts.

## What is communication apprehension and how can I reduce it?

Communication apprehension (CA) is the fear or anxiety associated with real or anticipated communication. It can vary in intensity and is influenced by trait CA (inherent anxiety) and state CA (contextual anxiety).

Symptoms include sweating, increased heart rate, and difficulty remembering content.

Strategies to reduce CA include:

- Practicing
- Relaxation techniques
- Systematic desensitization
- Visualization of success
- Treating the speech as a conversation

## What are presentation aids and how should I practice a speech?

Presentation aids enhance understanding and engagement. Types include:

- Objects: Physical items discussed in the speech.
- Models: Scaled representations of objects.
- Demonstrations: Enacting processes to teach the audience.
- Traditional aids: Charts, graphs, photographs, videos, and audio clips that support the speech.

Effective practice is essential for successful delivery. Practicing in front of a mirror, in front of small groups, or by recording sessions can help refine content and delivery. Important practice strategies are timing the speech, using presentation aids, and seeking feedback.

## What are the types of persuasive speaking and how do I organize a persuasive argument?

Persuasive speaking aims to change the attitudes, actions, or beliefs of others. It differs from informative speaking in its purpose.

Types of persuasion:

- Questions of fact: Change beliefs about what is true or false, as in debates over vaccines and climate change.
- Questions of value: Alter attitudes by arguing whether something is moral, ethical, or worthwhile, such as advocating for environmental protection.
- Questions of policy: Focus on actions that should or should not be taken, as in immigration reform or NCAA policies.
- Refutations: Defend a position while countering opposing arguments, which requires a deep understanding of the opponent's stance.

Ways to organize a persuasive argument:

- Problem-solution
- Problem-cause-solution
- Comparative advantages: Compare multiple solutions to argue for the best one.
- Monroe's Motivated Sequence: A five-step approach (attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action) to persuade an audience to take action.

## What are ethos, pathos, logos, and the common logical fallacies?

Persuasion uses emotion and logic:

- Ethos: The speaker's credibility, which can be enhanced through expertise and good delivery.
- Pathos: Emotional appeals that connect the audience to the topic, often through storytelling.
- Logos: Logical reasoning supported by evidence, using deductive and inductive reasoning.

Eleven common logical fallacies to avoid:

1. Ad hominem attack: Attacking the character instead of the argument.
2. Ad verecundiam: Relying on authority rather than logic.
3. Slippery slope: Assuming one action will lead to extreme consequences.
4. Non sequitur: Making unjustified leaps in reasoning.
5. Straw man: Misrepresenting an opponent's argument.
6. Hasty generalization: Drawing broad conclusions from limited evidence.
7. Either-or: Presenting only two extreme options.
8. False cause: Assuming causation from correlation.
9. Red herring: Distracting from the main issue.
10. Begging the question: Circular reasoning.
11. Appeal to pity: Manipulating emotions to gain agreement.

Dialogic persuasion means being open to new ideas, avoiding prejudgment, understanding different perspectives, and accepting disagreement.
